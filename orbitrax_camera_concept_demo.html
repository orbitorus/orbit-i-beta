Seeing what ground radar can't: fast, faint objects in orbit

A dim 5 cm object crosses the field too quickly for any single exposure to capture — long exposures smear it into noise. Our photon-counting sensor records thousands of instant frames instead, and software re-aligns them afterwards at any candidate speed. When the guess matches the object's true motion, it snaps into focus. The star field, kept sharp in a parallel projection of the same data, anchors the detection to sky coordinates.

Gauss gives a fast closed-form first guess by solving one polynomial for range; Gooding then iterates that guess with a Lambert solver until the predicted line-of-sight matches the middle observation. Real accuracy (per published star-tracker studies): semimajor axis and position good to a few hundred km, inclination reliable mainly at high inclination, eccentricity and RAAN weakly constrained from three observations alone.

Synthetic data, illustrative values. The pan speed that sharpens the object is the velocity measurement — detection and motion estimate are one computation. Three such measurements are sufficient for initial orbit determination (Gauss / Gooding), enabling a match against the object catalogue.

Honest data flow: each observation gives angles only — sky position (RA/Dec) and angular rate (how fast that position is changing, e.g. deg/s along a position angle). It does not give range or range-rate; an optical sensor cannot measure distance directly. Range is solved for, not measured — angles-only orbit determination (Gauss / Gooding) combines three or more such observations under two-body dynamics to infer the one range at each observation time consistent with a valid orbit. Full state (position, velocity, range) is a downstream product of ≥3 measurements, never a single frame.
